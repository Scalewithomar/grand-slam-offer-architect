# Buyer Definition & Market Selection

Sources: *$100M Offers* "The Lost Chapter" (Your First Avatar) + Section II
(Finding the Right Market). Use in Parts 1 and 2.

## Rule zero: you are channeling demand, not creating it

You cannot create desire; you can only channel existing demand. **A strong offer
is not for everyone — it's for a specific person in a specific situation.** "My
customer is everyone" must be rejected: an offer that speaks to everyone speaks
deeply to no one.

## The Lost Chapter avatar method (find your best buyer on purpose)

Vista Equity's playbook, adapted: analyze current customers, find the ones who
**stay longest and pay most**, and double down on the channels that bring them.
Pareto on steroids — the top 20% of customers often drive ~80% of revenue; replace
the bottom 80% with more top-spenders and you can grow the business ~5x.

**Four steps to install it:**

1. **Survey your customers.** Ask everything relevant. Question buckets:
   - **Demographics:** age, gender, location (physical + digital), marital/business
     status, solo vs partnered, etc.
   - **Business/personal stats — before & current:** revenue, profit, team size,
     churn, pricing, products, LTV, # customers, niche, tenure.
   - **Aspirations:** what goal were they chasing when they bought? What problem
     were they solving?
   - **Buying process:** the single biggest reason they bought; was there a
     **Trigger Event**; what content/testimonial did they consume; how many pieces;
     how long from first-hear to purchase; where did they first see you; referral?
2. **Find your biggest spenders.** Sort by customers you like most + who spent
   most + stayed longest. **Focus on the top 20%. Ignore the rest.**
3. **See what they have in common.** Read the answers and think. Goal: the
   **fewest qualifiers they all share** — usually **3 to 5**.
4. **Execute — two moves:**
   - **Speak to the new avatar.** Be upfront about customer requirements; make all
     advertising speak directly to them. This repels bad customers and attracts
     good ones. Stop selling anyone who doesn't meet the ideal.
   - **Re-engineer the sales process.** Reverse-engineer the buying journey your
     best customers actually went through, then make it happen on purpose. (Gym
     Launch found 78% of top customers consumed ≥2 long-form content pieces before
     buying → they injected two value-in-advance pieces into every lead's journey.)

**Quality > Quantity.** Every time qualification steps were removed, lead *volume*
rose but *money* fell. Prefer "pay $5,000 to acquire $45,000" over "pay $1,000 to
acquire $5,000." Narrowing serves fewer clients short-term (a dip during the cost
of change) but yields higher retention, margins, premium pricing, and repeat
business long-term. This step is a **force multiplier** on everything else — and
almost no one does it.

## The four market indicators (pick the right pond)

Before the offer, make sure the market is viable. Look for:

1. **Massive Pain** — they don't merely *want*, they *desperately need* it. Degree
   of pain ∝ price you can charge. *"The pain is the pitch."* If you articulate the
   pain accurately, they almost always buy.
2. **Purchasing Power** — they can afford it (the résumé coach failed because his
   market was *unemployed*). Target people with the money or access to it.
3. **Easy to Target** — reachable audiences/lists/channels. Hyper-local beats
   broad for local businesses (Towson, not Baltimore; Hinsdale, not Chicago).
4. **Growing** — not shrinking. (Lloyd's great offer failed selling to newspapers,
   a market shrinking 25%/yr; the same skills won in masks during COVID.)

## The three evergreen markets

**Health, Wealth, Relationships** — always in demand because lacking them is
always painful. Find a **subgroup** inside one that is growing, has buying power,
and is easy to target. (A relationship expert should prefer "second-half-of-life"
coaching for seniors — more pain, more money, easy to find, and a growing cohort —
over broke college students.)

## Order of importance (three levers on success)

> **Starving Crowd (market) > Offer Strength > Persuasion Skills**

A "great" on a higher lever overpowers weakness below it; a "bad" on a higher lever
stops everything unless a "great" above nullifies it. You can't be in a *bad*
market. Most readers are in a **normal** market — which is fine: a Grand Slam Offer
in a normal market makes tons of money even with weak persuasion. That's the whole
point of building the offer.

## Commit to the niche ("don't make me niche-slap you")

- Trying one offer once, failing, and blaming the market is the common error —
  usually you just haven't found the Grand Slam Offer yet.
- Dentists *and* chiropractors are both billion-dollar normal markets — either
  works, but **pick one.** No one serves two masters.
- **Riches are in the niches.** Under ~$10M/yr, niching down makes more money
  (fewer clients, more narrowly, higher-converting messaging). Companies have hit
  $30M+ in a single niche (chiropractors, gyms, plumbers, solar, roofers, salons).
  Broaden only above ~$10M and only as your TAM demands (up-market, down-market, or
  adjacent).

## Applying this in the workflow

- **Part 1:** confirm the market isn't shrinking; sanity-check pain + purchasing
  power for the chosen offer level.
- **Part 2:** derive Qualifiers/Disqualifiers from the top-20% commonalities (or,
  if no customers yet, from the *dream* best customer). Nail the **Trigger Event** —
  it's what makes them search *now*, and it's a direct input to Hooks in Part 11.
